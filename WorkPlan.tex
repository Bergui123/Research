\section{Goal}\label{sec:goal}
% =====================================================================

\textbf{Research question.} \emph{Fault-Tolerant Resource Estimation for
Schr\"odingerised Hamilton--Jacobi Equations: Logical Qubit and T-Gate Counts
for Multi-Asset Optimal Execution.}

\textbf{Deliverable.} A formula and a table giving the number of logical qubits
$N_q$ and T-gates $N_T$ needed to solve the two-asset optimal execution problem on
a fault-tolerant quantum computer, as functions of the grid size, the target
precision $\varepsilon$, the horizon $T$, and the Almgren--Chriss parameters
($\lambda$, the covariance matrix $C$, the impact matrix $H$). Every parameter in
the count must be traceable to a numbered equation in Almgren and Chriss, or
explicitly marked as derived in these notes.

\textbf{Why this chain of formulas, end to end.} Each arrow below is forced by a
specific mathematical need, not a stylistic choice:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item The Almgren--Chriss \emph{discrete, finite-sum} objective
        (Eqs.~15, 29, 30) is a static calculus-of-variations problem: it has no
        time-evolving ``state,'' so it cannot be fed to a time-stepping quantum
        simulation directly. It must first become a \textbf{running-cost
        integral} (Stage~2, Part~I of the integral derivation).
  \item A running-cost integral with free control $\mathbf v(t)$ is exactly the
        setting of \textbf{Bellman's dynamic programming principle}, which is
        the standard and essentially only systematic way to turn a minimisation
        over trajectories into a \textbf{PDE} satisfied by the value function
        $\mathcal V(\mathbf x,t)$ (Stage~2, Part~II).
  \item That PDE is a \textbf{Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman (HJB) equation}, which is
        nonlinear (it has $(\nabla\mathcal V)^\top H^{-1}\nabla\mathcal V$, a
        quadratic-in-gradient term). Quantum Hamiltonian simulation methods
        (qubitization, QSVT, Trotterisation) are built for \textbf{linear}
        evolution equations, so the nonlinearity must be removed before any
        quantum algorithm applies. The standard tool for removing a
        quadratic-in-gradient nonlinearity is the \textbf{Hopf--Cole
        transform} (Stage~4), which is why it appears here rather than, e.g.,
        a direct discretisation of the HJB equation.
  \item After Hopf--Cole, the equation is linear but still \textbf{not unitary}
        (it is a diffusion-type equation, $\partial_s\psi=-\hat H\psi$ with
        $\hat H\ge0$, so the semigroup $e^{-s\hat H}$ shrinks norms rather than
        rotating). Quantum computers natively implement unitary evolution.
        \textbf{Schr\"odingerisation} (Jin--Liu--Yu; Stage~7) is the specific
        published technique for embedding a non-unitary linear evolution into a
        unitary one on an enlarged space, which is why it is the next step
        rather than, e.g., ad-hoc normalisation.
  \item Once the equation is unitary, qubit and T-gate counting is a standard
        exercise in Hamiltonian-simulation complexity (qubitization/QSVT) plus a
        standard circuit cost for the discretised operator (finite differences
        and a quadratic term), which is Stage~8.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What the paper does not claim.} It does not propose a new trading
strategy, and it does not claim a quantum speedup. For this linear-quadratic
problem the classical solution is a closed-form Riccati ODE (Stage~2). The
contribution is the cost of running the full pipeline on a benchmark instance,
reported honestly, including the parts not yet checked.

\section{Notation and background concepts}\label{sec:notation}
% =====================================================================

This section explains, in plain terms, the mathematical objects and operators
used repeatedly from Stage~1 onward. It does not change any formula; it is a
reference to consult whenever a symbol or concept in a later stage is unclear.

\subsection*{Vectors and matrices}

\textbf{Bold symbols} ($\mathbf x$, $\mathbf v$) are vectors: ordered lists of
numbers, one entry per asset. Here $\mathbf x=(x_1,x_2)$ for two assets means
``$x_1$ shares of asset 1, $x_2$ shares of asset 2'' held at some instant.
\textbf{Capital letters} ($H$, $C$, $P$) are matrices: square tables of
numbers that act on vectors by matrix multiplication. $\mathbf x^\top$ means
``$\mathbf x$ written as a row instead of a column''; $\mathbf x^\top C\mathbf
x$ (a ``quadratic form'') is a single number computed from $\mathbf x$ and
$C$, expanding to $\sum_{a,b}C_{ab}x_ax_b$. It behaves like a generalised
sum-of-squares: if $C$ is the identity matrix, $\mathbf x^\top C\mathbf x$ is
just $x_1^2+x_2^2$.

\textbf{Diagonal matrix}, $H=\mathrm{diag}(\eta_1,\eta_2)$, has $\eta_1,\eta_2$
on the diagonal and zero everywhere else; multiplying a vector by it just
scales each entry independently ($H\mathbf v=(\eta_1v_1,\eta_2v_2)$). This is
why a diagonal $H$ means ``asset~1's cost and asset~2's cost don't interact.''

\textbf{Symmetric positive (semi)definite} ($C=C^\top$, and $\mathbf x^\top
C\mathbf x\ge0$ for every $\mathbf x$) is the matrix generalisation of ``a
positive number.'' It guarantees $\mathbf x^\top C\mathbf x$ behaves like a
genuine squared-size measure (never negative), which is required for it to
represent a variance (variances cannot be negative).

\textbf{Eigenvalues and eigenvectors.} For a matrix $M$, an eigenvector
$\mathbf u$ is a direction that $M$ only stretches, not rotates:
$M\mathbf u=\mu\,\mathbf u$ for some number $\mu$ (the eigenvalue). Any
symmetric matrix can be written as $M=U\,\mathrm{diag}(\mu_i)\,U^\top$, where
$U$'s columns are the eigenvectors and $\mathrm{diag}(\mu_i)$ holds the
eigenvalues --- this is called \textbf{diagonalisation}. It matters here
because once you rotate into the eigenvector coordinates, a matrix that
mixed the two assets together becomes two independent scalar problems, each
easy to solve on its own (used in Stage~2.II.6 and Stage~5).

\subsection*{Calculus objects: gradient, divergence, Laplacian}

$\nabla\mathcal V$ (``grad $\mathcal V$'' or ``the gradient'') is the vector of
partial derivatives of a scalar function $\mathcal V(\mathbf x)$ with respect
to each coordinate: $\nabla\mathcal V=(\partial\mathcal V/\partial x_1,
\partial\mathcal V/\partial x_2)$. It points in the direction $\mathcal V$
increases fastest, and its size tells you how sensitive the cost is to a small
change in holdings.

$\nabla\!\cdot\!(\cdot)$ (``divergence'') takes a vector field and returns a
scalar measuring net outward flow; applied to $H^{-1}\nabla W$ it produces the
(weighted) \textbf{Laplacian}, the multi-dimensional second derivative. The
Laplacian is the standard operator describing diffusion/spreading-out (as in
the heat equation); its appearance in Stage~4's viscosity term is literally
adding a small amount of smoothing/diffusion to the equation.

\subsection*{PDEs, boundary/terminal conditions, and ``running backward''}

A PDE (partial differential equation) is an equation relating a function's
value to its own derivatives, here in both $\mathbf x$ (space: inventory) and
$t$ (time). It needs a condition fixing the function at one edge of the time
range to pin down a unique solution. Here that condition is given at the
\emph{end} of the horizon ($t=T$, inventory must be zero), not the start, which
is why the PDE in Stage~2 (Eq.~\eqref{eq:pde}) is said to ``run backward'':
you know the answer at $t=T$ and solve for earlier times, the reverse of how
an ordinary simulation (knowing the start, solving for the future) runs. Stage~4's
first step (time reversal $s=T-t$) exists purely to flip this around so a
forward-running numerical/quantum simulation can be used.

\subsection*{The Riccati equation}

A \textbf{Riccati equation} is an ODE where the unknown appears squared (here,
matrix-squared: $P H^{-1}P$). It shows up whenever you minimise a quadratic
cost with linear dynamics (the ``linear-quadratic control'' setting), which is
exactly this problem's structure (quadratic cost in Stage~1, linear inventory
dynamics $\dot{\mathbf x}=-\mathbf v$). Unlike a generic nonlinear ODE, this
particular one has a known closed-form solution in terms of $\sinh$/$\cosh$
when, as here, the coefficients don't depend on time --- which is exactly the
Stage~2 benchmark trajectory.

\subsection*{Hermitian and unitary operators (why quantum computing cares)}

A matrix (or operator) is \textbf{Hermitian} if it equals its own conjugate
transpose; for the real matrices in this project, that just means
\emph{symmetric} ($M=M^\top$). Hermitian operators are important because
quantum mechanics requires the operator generating time evolution (``the
Hamiltonian,'' $\hat H$ or $H_S$ here) to be Hermitian --- it is what
guarantees the evolution conserves total probability.

A matrix is \textbf{unitary} if multiplying by it preserves vector length
(it is a generalised rotation, never a stretch or shrink). A quantum computer
physically can only apply unitary operations to its qubits. The
Schr\"odinger equation $i\partial_s\hat w=H_S\hat w$ with $H_S$ Hermitian has
the solution $\hat w(s)=e^{-isH_S}\hat w(0)$, and $e^{-isH_S}$ is
\emph{automatically} unitary whenever $H_S$ is Hermitian --- this is the exact
reason Stage~4--7 go to the trouble of turning the original (non-Hermitian in
structure, and non-unitary in evolution) HJB equation into something of this
Hermitian/unitary form: it's the only form a quantum computer can run
natively.

By contrast, the equation after Hopf--Cole alone, $\partial_s\psi=-\hat
H\psi$, has solution $\psi(s)=e^{-s\hat H}\psi(0)$. Even with $\hat H$
Hermitian, the operator $e^{-s\hat H}$ (no factor of $i$) is \emph{not}
unitary when $\hat H\ge0$ --- it shrinks vector length over time rather than
rotating it (this is ordinary exponential decay, like heat spreading out and
cooling). That mismatch -- Hermitian but not unitary evolution -- is precisely
the gap Schr\"odingerisation (Stage~7) is built to close.

\subsection*{Qubits, registers, and gate counts}

A \textbf{qubit} is the quantum unit of information; $n$ qubits together can
represent (a quantum superposition of) any of $2^n$ distinct numbers at once.
A \textbf{register} is a group of qubits dedicated to representing one
quantity --- here, $n_x$ qubits per asset represent that asset's position on a
grid of $2^{n_x}$ possible values (Stage~6).

Any quantum computation is built from a sequence of elementary \textbf{gates}.
On a fault-tolerant (error-corrected) quantum computer, most gates
(rotations, CNOTs) are considered free/cheap, but the \textbf{T-gate}
(a specific single-qubit gate) is expensive to implement reliably, so the
\textbf{T-count} ($N_T$) --- the total number of T-gates in the whole
circuit --- is the standard measure of ``how expensive is this computation,''
analogous to counting the slowest, most resource-hungry instruction in an
otherwise fast process. A \textbf{Toffoli gate} (a 3-qubit AND-like gate,
heavily used for arithmetic such as the multiplications in Stage~8) itself
costs a known fixed number of T-gates to implement fault-tolerantly, which is
why gate counts for arithmetic circuits are usually first counted in Toffolis
and then converted to T-gates.

\subsection*{Big-O notation}

$O(f(\varepsilon))$ means ``bounded above by a constant times $f(\varepsilon)$,
for $\varepsilon$ small (or whatever variable is named)'' --- it describes how
a cost \emph{scales} as a parameter changes, while deliberately hiding the
exact constant multiplier. It is used throughout Stage~8--9 because the
precise constants depend on which specific circuit construction is chosen
(still to be pinned down by reading the cited papers), but the \emph{scaling
behaviour} (e.g.\ ``cost grows linearly in $T$'' vs.\ ``quadratically in
$1/\varepsilon$'') is already known and is what determines whether a method is
competitive.

\subsection*{Amplitude vs.\ probability, and amplitude estimation}

In quantum mechanics, a state is a (complex) vector of \textbf{amplitudes},
one per possible outcome; the \emph{probability} of actually observing a given
outcome on measurement is the \emph{square} of its amplitude's size. A single
measurement only ever reveals one random outcome, sampled according to those
probabilities --- it never reveals an amplitude directly. \textbf{Amplitude
estimation} (Stage~9) is an algorithm that runs a quantum circuit repeatedly
in a structured way to estimate an amplitude itself (not just sample from the
probabilities it implies) to a chosen precision, using fewer repetitions than
plain repeated sampling would need for the same precision.

\section{Stage 1 --- Repair the reference document and fix the starting formula}
\label{sec:stage1}
% =====================================================================

\subsection*{What this stage needs}

Nothing upstream: this is the entry point. It needs only a copy of Almgren and
Chriss, \emph{Optimal Execution of Portfolio Transactions} (Dec.\ 2000), and the
existing (defective) reference LaTeX document.

\subsection*{How it was achieved}

The multi-asset trading cost in Almgren--Chriss is built from three separate
results in the paper, combined into one objective:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Expected cost of a schedule} (their Eq.~8, single asset,
        generalised to Eq.~29 for $M$ assets): a sum of (i) a permanent-impact
        term $\tfrac12\gamma_jX_j^2$ that depends only on the total size
        liquidated, not on the schedule, (ii) a spread cost
        $\varepsilon_j\sum_k|n_{jk}|$, and (iii) a temporary-impact
        (``slippage'') term $\frac{\tilde\eta_j}{\tau}\sum_k n_{jk}^2$ that
        penalises large trades in a short interval. The coefficient
        $\tilde\eta_j=\eta_j-\tfrac12\gamma_j\tau$ (not $\eta_j$ itself) is the
        one that appears, because part of the instantaneous impact is already
        counted inside the permanent-impact term; this is exactly the
        correction worked out in the paper's Sec.~1.5.
  \item \textbf{Variance of the cost} (their Eq.~5, generalised to Eq.~30): the
        quadratic form $\lambda\sum_k\tau\,\mathbf x_k^\top C\mathbf x_k$, where
        $C=\sigma\sigma^\top$ is the covariance matrix of the asset return
        shocks. This term exists because the execution cost is random (it
        depends on the realised price path), and a risk-averse trader cares
        about the variance of that randomness, not only its mean.
  \item \textbf{Mean--variance combination} (their Eq.~15): $U=E+\lambda V$. This
        is the paper's definition of ``the efficient frontier'' --- the
        objective a trader with risk-aversion $\lambda$ minimises is expected
        cost plus $\lambda$ times variance, exactly as in Markowitz
        mean-variance portfolio theory.
\end{enumerate}

Putting these together, for diagonal permanent- and temporary-impact matrices
(their Eq.~32, the case this project restricts to):

\begin{equation}
U[\mathbf{x}]
= \sum_{j=1}^{M}\left[
    \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma_j X_j^2
    + \varepsilon_j \sum_{k=1}^{N}|n_{jk}|
    + \frac{\tilde{\eta}_j}{\tau}\sum_{k=1}^{N} n_{jk}^2
    \right]
+ \lambda \sum_{k=1}^{N} \tau\,\mathbf{x}_k^\top C\,\mathbf{x}_k
\label{eq:s1-objective}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Which formula is used, and why}

Equation~\eqref{eq:s1-objective} and not some other objective (e.g.\ worst-case
cost, or a pure-mean objective) is used because:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item it is the objective Almgren and Chriss actually solve in closed form for
        a single asset (giving the benchmark trajectory used in Stage~2 as a
        correctness check), so adopting it lets every later stage be validated
        against a known answer;
  \item the mean--variance form is quadratic in $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf v$,
        which is exactly what turns the resulting PDE into a solvable matrix
        Riccati equation (Stage~2) and what makes the Hopf--Cole transform
        (Stage~4) applicable in closed form. A more general risk measure
        (e.g.\ CVaR) would not have this property and would block the entire
        downstream pipeline.
\end{itemize}

\section{Stage 2 --- Lock the classical benchmark}
\label{sec:stage2}
% =====================================================================

\subsection*{What this stage needs}

The discrete objective~\eqref{eq:s1-objective} from Stage~1, and the sales-only
and diagonal-impact assumptions (A6, A7 in Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}) so
that the continuum limit below is valid.

\subsection*{How it was achieved --- Part I: from the discrete sum to an integral}

\paragraph{I.1 Drop the terms that do not depend on the schedule.}
$\tfrac12\gamma_jX_j^2$ is fixed by the number of shares to liquidate; it cannot
be changed by choosing a different schedule. $\varepsilon_j\sum_k|n_{jk}|=
\varepsilon_jX_j$ \emph{provided every trade is a sale} ($n_{jk}\ge0$, A7); then
it too is schedule-independent. A constant added to the cost does not change
which schedule is cheapest, so both drop out:
\begin{equation}
  U'[\mathbf{x}] = \sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{\tilde{\eta}_j}{\tau}\sum_{k=1}^{N} n_{jk}^2
  + \lambda \sum_{k=1}^{N} \tau\,\mathbf{x}_k^\top C\,\mathbf{x}_k
\end{equation}

\paragraph{I.2 Replace shares-per-interval by a selling rate.}
Shares sold in an interval equal rate times interval length,
$n_{jk}=\tau\,v_j(t_k)$, so
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\tilde\eta_j}{\tau}\,n_{jk}^2=\frac{\tilde\eta_j}{\tau}\,\tau^2 v_j^2
  =\tilde\eta_j\,\tau\,v_j(t_k)^2 .
\end{equation}
Both terms of $U'$ now have the form $\tau\times(\text{something at }t_k)$,
summed over $k$: the $1/\tau$ in the original coefficient is exactly what makes
this rewriting possible.

\paragraph{I.3 The sum over time becomes an integral.}
A sum $\sum_k \tau f(t_k)$ is, by definition, a Riemann sum for $f$ on $[0,T]$,
so letting $\tau\to0$ (equivalently $N\to\infty$) gives
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{k=1}^{N}\tau\,f(t_k)\;\xrightarrow[\tau\to0]{}\;\int_0^T f(t)\,dt .
\end{equation}
This is the \emph{only} mechanism used to pass from discrete to continuous time:
no further approximation is introduced.

\paragraph{I.4 The sum over assets becomes a matrix product.}
The sum over $j$ is not removed, only written compactly. With
$H=\mathrm{diag}(\tilde\eta_1,\dots,\tilde\eta_M)$:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j=1}^{M}\tilde\eta_j\,v_j^2=\mathbf{v}^\top H\,\mathbf{v},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{x}^\top C\,\mathbf{x}=\sum_{a,b=1}^{M}C_{ab}\,x_a x_b .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{I.5 The inventory update becomes a derivative.}
$\mathbf{x}_k=\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{n}_k$, divided by $\tau$ and taken to the
limit, gives $\dot{\mathbf{x}}=-\mathbf{v}$: the inventory decreases at exactly
the selling rate. The boundary conditions are the full position at the start and
a flat position at the horizon.

\paragraph{I.6 Result.}
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  J[\mathbf{v}]
  = \int_0^T \Big( \mathbf{v}(t)^\top H\,\mathbf{v}(t)
      + \lambda\,\mathbf{x}(t)^\top C\,\mathbf{x}(t) \Big)\,dt,
  \quad \dot{\mathbf{x}}=-\mathbf{v},\quad
  \mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{X},\quad \mathbf{x}(T)=\mathbf{0}\;}
  \label{eq:J}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Why the Riemann-sum limit and not, e.g., a direct continuous-time
postulate.} Taking the limit from the discrete objective (rather than simply
writing down a plausible continuous-time cost) is what lets every later stage
be checked against the discrete problem directly: Stage~2's checks below
compare the continuum solution to a \emph{direct numerical minimisation of the
discrete objective}~\eqref{eq:s1-objective} at finite $N$, and the two must
agree as $N$ grows. This is the chosen correctness criterion for the whole
pipeline.

\subsection*{How it was achieved --- Part II: from the integral to the PDE and its solution}

\paragraph{II.1 Define the cost-to-go.}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x},t)=\min_{\mathbf{v}(\cdot)}\int_t^T
  \Big(\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v}+\lambda\,\mathbf{x}^\top C\mathbf{x}\Big)\,ds,
  \qquad \mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{x}.
\end{equation}
This is the cheapest possible remaining cost given inventory $\mathbf x$ at
time $t$. It is introduced because the integral~\eqref{eq:J} by itself is a
single number for a single trajectory; to get an equation that \emph{evolves},
the cost must be made a function of the current state and time.

\paragraph{II.2 Apply Bellman's principle.}
\textbf{Why Bellman and not, e.g., Euler--Lagrange.} Both are valid for this
problem, but Bellman's principle produces a PDE in $(\mathbf x,t)$ directly ---
exactly the object a Hamiltonian-simulation quantum algorithm needs (a linear
operator acting on a function of $\mathbf x$, evolved in $t$). Euler--Lagrange
would give a two-point boundary-value ODE for the optimal trajectory only, with
no state-space PDE to Schr\"odingerise. Splitting the horizon into a short step
$dt$ and the rest:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x},t)=\min_{\mathbf{v}}\Big[\big(\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v}
  +\lambda\,\mathbf{x}^\top C\mathbf{x}\big)dt+\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v}\,dt,\;t+dt)\Big].
\end{equation}
Expanding the last term to first order,
$\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v}\,dt,t+dt)\approx\mathcal{V}+\partial_t\mathcal{V}\,dt-\nabla\mathcal{V}\cdot\mathbf{v}\,dt$,
and cancelling $\mathcal{V}$ gives
\begin{equation}
  -\partial_t\mathcal{V}=\min_{\mathbf{v}}\Big[\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v}
  +\lambda\,\mathbf{x}^\top C\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v}^\top\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{V}\Big].
\end{equation}

\paragraph{II.3 Minimise over the control.}
The bracket is quadratic (convex, since $H\succ0$, A11) in $\mathbf{v}$, so the
minimiser is found by setting the gradient in $\mathbf v$ to zero --- this is
the step that turns a \emph{minimisation} into an explicit \emph{formula}:
\begin{equation}
  2H\mathbf{v}-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{V}=0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \mathbf{v}^*(\mathbf{x},t)=\tfrac12H^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{V}.
  \label{eq:optcontrol}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{II.4 Substitute back: the Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman (HJB) equation.}
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\partial_t\mathcal{V}-\tfrac14(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{V})^\top H^{-1}
  (\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{V})+\lambda\,\mathbf{x}^\top C\,\mathbf{x}=0\;}
  \label{eq:pde}
\end{equation}
This runs backward from $t=T$. The terminal condition is $\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x},T)=0$
for $\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ and $+\infty$ otherwise (A10); in practice this is
implemented as a penalty $\tfrac12\mathbf{x}^\top\Phi\,\mathbf{x}$ with $\Phi$
large.

\paragraph{II.5 Solve by the quadratic (Riccati) ansatz, and why that ansatz.}
The PDE~\eqref{eq:pde} is quadratic in $\mathbf x$ in every term (the gradient
term is quadratic in $\nabla\mathcal V$, which is linear in $\mathbf x$ if
$\mathcal V$ is quadratic; the running-cost term is quadratic in $\mathbf x$
directly). A quadratic form is therefore a consistent ansatz, and it is also
the \emph{simplest} ansatz that can satisfy a terminal condition that is itself
quadratic ($\Phi$). Try
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal V=\tfrac12\mathbf x^\top P(t)\mathbf x,\qquad \nabla\mathcal V = P(t)\mathbf x.
\end{equation}
Substituting into~\eqref{eq:pde} and matching the resulting quadratic forms in
$\mathbf x$ (since the equation must hold for every $\mathbf x$) gives the
\textbf{matrix Riccati equation}:
\begin{equation}
  \dot P=\tfrac12\,P\,H^{-1}P-2\lambda C,\qquad P(T)=\Phi .
  \label{eq:ric}
\end{equation}
Substituting $\mathcal V=\tfrac12\mathbf x^\top P\mathbf x$ into the optimal
control~\eqref{eq:optcontrol} gives the optimal feedback law
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf v^*=\tfrac12 H^{-1}P\,\mathbf x .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{II.6 Diagonalise to solve the Riccati equation in closed form.}
\textbf{Why diagonalise.} Equation~\eqref{eq:ric} is a matrix ODE coupling all
components of $P$; it has no elementary closed form in general. But because
$H$ is diagonal and positive definite (A2, A11) and $C$ is symmetric positive
semidefinite (A13), the matrix $H^{-1/2}CH^{-1/2}$ is symmetric and can be
diagonalised by an orthogonal matrix $U$:
\begin{equation}
  H^{-1/2}CH^{-1/2}=U\,\mathrm{diag}(\mu_i)\,U^\top .
\end{equation}
In the rotated, rescaled coordinates $\mathbf y=U^\top H^{1/2}\mathbf x$ the
problem decouples into $M$ independent scalar Riccati equations, each of the
same form as the exactly-solvable one-asset problem, with natural frequency
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_i=\sqrt{\lambda\mu_i}.
\end{equation}
Each scalar Riccati equation with a flat terminal condition has the standard
closed-form solution in terms of $\sinh$ and $\cosh$ (this is the one-asset
Almgren--Chriss result, their Eq.~17, applied componentwise). Undoing the
rotation gives the trajectory
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}(t) = H^{-1/2}U\,\mathrm{diag}\!\Big(\frac{\sinh\kappa_i(T-t)}
      {\sinh\kappa_iT}\Big)U^\top H^{1/2}\mathbf{X}
  \label{eq:traj}
\end{equation}
and the optimal cost $\mathcal V(\mathbf{X},0)=\tfrac12\mathbf{X}^\top P(0)\mathbf{X}$.

\section{Stage 4 --- Time-to-go form and Cole--Hopf (linearisation)}
\label{sec:stage4}
% =====================================================================

\subsection*{What this stage needs}

The HJB equation~\eqref{eq:pde} from Stage~2 and its boundary data (the penalty
$\Phi$ from Stage~3 / the assumptions register). It does \emph{not} need
Stage~3's numerical instance yet; the transform is instance-independent.

\subsection*{Why this stage exists}

As explained in Stage~0, the HJB equation~\eqref{eq:pde} is nonlinear in
$\nabla\mathcal V$, and quantum Hamiltonian-simulation methods only evolve
\emph{linear} operators. The Hopf--Cole transform is the standard device for
removing a nonlinearity of the specific form $(\nabla\mathcal V)^\top
M\nabla\mathcal V$ (quadratic in the gradient, with a constant matrix $M$) by
an exponential substitution; it is used here for exactly that reason, and for
no other nonlinearity type.

\subsection*{How it was achieved}

\paragraph{Step 1: reverse time.} The HJB equation runs backward from $t=T$;
Hamiltonian simulation runs forward from $s=0$. Define
$s=T-t$, $W(\mathbf x,s)=\mathcal V(\mathbf x,T-s)$, which turns the terminal
condition at $t=T$ into an initial condition at $s=0$.

\paragraph{Step 2: add a viscosity term.} The Hopf--Cole substitution below
requires the equation to have a diffusion term for the exponential map to
cancel the nonlinearity exactly (this is the matrix generalisation of the
classical Hopf--Cole trick for Burgers' equation, where a viscosity term is
likewise required). Since the original HJB equation~\eqref{eq:pde} has no
diffusion term, one is added with a small coefficient $\nu>0$:
\begin{equation}
  \partial_sW+\tfrac14\nabla W^\top H^{-1}\nabla W=\lambda\,\mathbf x^\top C\mathbf x
    +\nu\,\nabla\!\cdot\!(H^{-1}\nabla W),\qquad W(\mathbf x,0)=\tfrac12\mathbf x^\top\Phi\mathbf x
  \label{eq:viscous}
\end{equation}
This is a regularisation, not a change of problem: Stage~4's key claim (below)
shows $\nu$ only shifts an additive constant in the cost, not the optimal
trading rate, so the original control problem is recovered for every $\nu>0$.

\paragraph{Step 3: the Hopf--Cole substitution.} Set
\begin{equation}
  \psi=e^{-W/(4\nu)}.
\end{equation}
\textbf{Why this exponent and not, e.g., $e^{-W/\nu}$ or $e^{-W/(2\nu)}$:} the
factor $4\nu$ is fixed by requiring the quadratic term in the transformed
equation to cancel exactly. Differentiating,
\begin{equation}
  \nabla\psi=-\frac{\nabla W}{4\nu}\psi
  \;\Longrightarrow\;
  \nabla W=-4\nu\,\nabla\ln\psi,
  \qquad
  \partial_s\psi=-\frac{\partial_sW}{4\nu}\psi .
\end{equation}
Substituting into~\eqref{eq:viscous} and expanding $\nabla\!\cdot\!(H^{-1}\nabla
W)$ in terms of $\psi$ produces a term $\propto(\nabla W)^\top H^{-1}\nabla W$
from the diffusion part that is exactly $-4\nu$ times the original nonlinear
term; with the coefficient $1/(4\nu)$ chosen above, these two contributions
cancel, leaving a \textbf{linear} equation:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\partial_s\psi=-\hat H\psi,\qquad
  \hat H=-\nu\,\nabla\!\cdot\!(H^{-1}\nabla)+\frac{\lambda}{4\nu}\,\mathbf x^\top C\mathbf x\;}
  \label{eq:linearised}
\end{equation}
This is a linear diffusion (heat-type) equation with a quadratic potential ---
structurally identical to a Schr\"odinger equation in imaginary time, with
$\hat H$ playing the role of a Hamiltonian (hence the notation).

\paragraph{Step 4: recover the control.} From
$\mathbf v^*=\tfrac12H^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf x}\mathcal V$
(Stage~2, Eq.~\eqref{eq:optcontrol}) and $\nabla W=-4\nu\nabla\ln\psi$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf v^*=-2\nu\,H^{-1}\nabla\ln\psi .
\end{equation}
So the entire point of solving~\eqref{eq:linearised} is to obtain
$\nabla\ln\psi$ at the current inventory, from which the trading rate follows
directly.

\subsection*{Which formula is used, and why (summary)}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{3.3cm}L{3.6cm}L{6.9cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Formula} & \textbf{Equation} & \textbf{Why this one} \\
\midrule
Time reversal $s=T-t$ & Step 1 & Converts a backward terminal-value problem
  into a forward initial-value problem, as required for simulation. \\
Added viscosity $\nu\nabla\!\cdot\!(H^{-1}\nabla W)$ & Step 2 & Only term whose
  expansion under the exponential map produces the exact quadratic-gradient
  term needed to cancel the HJB nonlinearity (matrix Hopf--Cole). \\
$\psi=e^{-W/(4\nu)}$ & Step 3 & The unique exponent making the cancellation
  exact; any other constant leaves a residual nonlinear term. \\
$\mathbf v^*=-2\nu H^{-1}\nabla\ln\psi$ & Step 4 & Direct substitution of the
  Cole--Hopf relation into the already-derived optimal feedback law
  \eqref{eq:optcontrol}; no new assumption. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection*{Key claim to verify}

Writing $W=\tfrac12\mathbf x^\top P(s)\mathbf x+c(s)$, the quadratic part must
obey \emph{the same} Riccati equation as~\eqref{eq:ric} (since adding a
constant-coefficient diffusion term to a quadratic PDE does not change the
quadratic part's evolution, only adds a trace term to the scalar part), and
$\dot c=\nu\,\mathrm{tr}(H^{-1}P)$. If true, $\nu$ changes only an additive
constant in $W$ (hence a multiplicative prefactor in $\psi$), not the gradient
$\nabla W$ and hence not the optimal control $\mathbf v^*$ --- which is why
$\nu$ is free to choose for numerical convenience and need not be taken to
zero.

\subsection*{Source}

The Hopf--Cole transform is standard (Evans, \emph{Partial Differential
Equations}, Hamilton--Jacobi / viscosity-solutions chapter); the matrix version
used here, with $H^{-1}$ in place of a scalar diffusivity, is derived in these
notes and is \textbf{not yet checked}.

\subsection*{Exit criterion}

Substituting $\psi=e^{-W/(4\nu)}$ back by hand reproduces
equation~\eqref{eq:linearised} exactly (algebra check); for one asset the
gradient $\nabla\ln\psi$ computed from the exact Gaussian solution of
\eqref{eq:linearised} (Stage~5) equals the Stage~2 gradient $P(t)x$, for every
$\nu$ tested.\\

\section{Stage 5 --- Spectrum and length scales; choosing $\nu$}
\label{sec:stage5}
% =====================================================================

\subsection*{What this stage needs}

The linear equation~\eqref{eq:linearised} from Stage~4, and the simultaneous
diagonalisation $H^{-1/2}CH^{-1/2}=U\,\mathrm{diag}(\mu_i)U^\top$ already
computed in Stage~2 (Eq.~II.6) --- the same decomposition is reused, not
recomputed independently, because $\hat H$ is built from the same $H$ and $C$.

\subsection*{How it was achieved}

\paragraph{Why this becomes a harmonic oscillator.} The potential term in
$\hat H$, $\frac{\lambda}{4\nu}\mathbf x^\top C\mathbf x$, is quadratic, and the
kinetic term $-\nu\nabla\!\cdot\!(H^{-1}\nabla)$ is a (possibly anisotropic)
Laplacian. An operator that is minus-a-Laplacian plus a quadratic potential is,
by definition, a quantum harmonic oscillator; diagonalising the quadratic form
in the same coordinates that diagonalise the Laplacian's metric turns it into a
\emph{sum of independent one-dimensional} harmonic oscillators, which have a
fully known closed-form spectrum. This is the reason the same $U,\mu_i$ from
Stage~2 reappear here.

With $\mathbf y=U^\top H^{1/2}\mathbf x$ (change of variables chosen precisely
to simultaneously diagonalise the kinetic metric $H^{-1}$ and the potential
matrix $C$), the operator $\hat H$ splits into independent oscillators:
\begin{equation}
  \hat H=\sum_i\Big[-\nu\,\partial_{y_i}^2+\frac{\lambda\mu_i}{4\nu}y_i^2\Big],\qquad
  E_{\mathbf n}=\sum_i\kappa_i\big(n_i+\tfrac12\big),\qquad
  \ell_i=\sqrt{2\nu/\kappa_i}
  \label{eq:oscillator}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_i=\sqrt{\lambda\mu_i}$ is the \emph{same} frequency computed in
Stage~2 and used again in Stage~3 --- a consistency check in itself, since the
Riccati solution's decay rate and the oscillator's ground-state energy must
agree (both come from the same eigenvalue problem).

\textbf{Why the spectrum does not depend on $\nu$ but $\ell_i$ does.} The
energy levels $E_{\mathbf n}$ of a harmonic oscillator depend only on the
frequency $\kappa_i$ (a ratio of potential strength to mass, here
$\lambda\mu_i$ to the kinetic coefficient --- the $\nu$'s in numerator and
denominator of the standard oscillator-frequency formula cancel). The
ground-state \emph{width} $\ell_i$, by contrast, is set by the balance between
kinetic and potential terms at fixed energy, and that balance scales with
$\sqrt\nu$. This is why $\nu$ is a free regularisation parameter for the
numerics (grid resolution) but not for the physics (decay rate) --- consistent
with the Stage~4 key claim that $\nu$ does not affect the optimal control.

The norm of $\psi$ decays like $e^{-E_0T}$ along the flow with
$E_0=\tfrac12\sum_i\kappa_i$ (the ground-state energy), since the general
solution is a superposition of oscillator eigenstates each decaying as
$e^{-E_{\mathbf n}s}$, and the ground state dominates for $s=T$ large relative
to $1/\kappa_i$.

\subsection*{Which formula is used, and why}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{3.3cm}L{3.6cm}L{6.9cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Formula} & \textbf{Equation} & \textbf{Why this one} \\
\midrule
Harmonic-oscillator decomposition & \eqref{eq:oscillator} & $\hat H$ is exactly
  minus-Laplacian-plus-quadratic-potential in the diagonalising coordinates;
  this is the standard, exactly solvable form, not an approximation. \\
$\kappa_i=\sqrt{\lambda\mu_i}$ reused from Stage~2 & --- & Same eigenvalue
  problem ($H^{-1/2}CH^{-1/2}$); reusing it is both a shortcut and a built-in
  consistency check between Stage~2 and Stage~5. \\
$\ell_i=\sqrt{2\nu/\kappa_i}$ & \eqref{eq:oscillator} & Standard oscillator
  ground-state width formula; the natural length scale the discretisation grid
  (Stage~6) must resolve. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection*{Decision to make}

$\nu$ trades two things against each other: $\psi$ at the starting inventory
$\mathbf X$ has size about $e^{-W(\mathbf X)/(4\nu)}$, so a \emph{small} $\nu$
makes $\psi(\mathbf X)$ tiny and the eventual amplitude-estimation readout
(Stage~9) expensive; while $\ell_i$ (growing with $\nu$) sets how fine the
simulation grid must be, so a \emph{large} $\nu$ relaxes the grid-resolution
requirement of Stage~6 but may push $\psi(\mathbf X)$ even smaller if $W(\mathbf
X)>0$ grows faster than $\nu$. The resolution is to sweep $\nu$ numerically
and tabulate the trade-off rather than fix it analytically, since the two
costs (readout vs.\ grid size) enter the final $N_q,N_T$ formulas with
different functional forms (Stages 6, 8, 9).

\subsection*{Source}

Derived here from the standard quantum harmonic oscillator; the spectrum and
eigenfunctions are textbook results (any quantum mechanics text), but their
application to this specific $\hat H$ is unchecked numerically.

A table of $\ell_i$, $E_0T$, and $\psi(\mathbf X)$ versus $\nu$ for the Stage~3
instance.\\

\section{Stage 6 --- Discretisation}
\label{sec:stage6}
% =====================================================================

\subsection*{What this stage needs}

The continuum operator $\hat H$ from Stage~4/5 and the length scales $\ell_i$
from Stage~5, which set the required grid spacing.

\subsection*{How it is to be achieved}

\paragraph{Grid choice.} Use $N_x=2^{n_x}$ grid points per asset on $[-L,L]$,
spacing $\Delta x=2L/N_x$. \textbf{Why a power of two:} this is the register
size a quantum computer encodes natively as $n_x$ qubits per asset, so the
classical grid and the quantum register are chosen jointly rather than
independently. \textbf{Why these bounds:} $L\ge\max_jX_j$ is required so the
classical trajectory (Stage~2) never leaves the simulated domain, and
$\Delta x$ small compared with $\min_i\ell_i$ (Stage~5) is required so the
discretisation resolves the ground-state width of the narrowest oscillator
mode --- under-resolving it would make the discrete ground state a poor
approximation to the continuum one, breaking the Stage~5 decay-rate estimate.

\paragraph{Discrete operator.} Replace the continuum Laplacian in $\hat H$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:linearised}) by a second-difference matrix $D_{2,a}$ per asset
and the continuum quadratic potential by its value on grid points:
\begin{equation}
  \hat H_d=\nu\sum_a\frac1{\eta_a}(-D_{2,a})+\frac{\lambda}{4\nu}\sum_{a,b}C_{ab}X_aX_b,
  \qquad
  \|\hat H_d\|\lesssim\frac{4\nu}{\Delta x^2}\sum_a\frac1{\eta_a}
     +\frac{\lambda L^2}{4\nu}\sum_{a,b}|C_{ab}|
  \label{eq:disc-operator}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Why a second-difference (central finite-difference) operator and not,
e.g., a spectral or finite-element discretisation.} A central second
difference is the standard choice when (i) the operator to discretise is
Hermitian (here, $-\nabla\cdot(H^{-1}\nabla)$, since $H^{-1}$ is a constant
diagonal positive matrix) and the discretisation must preserve Hermiticity for
the later Schr\"odingerisation step to produce a genuinely unitary evolution,
and (ii) a uniform grid is already fixed by the register structure above, so a
finite-difference stencil is the lowest-overhead option (one Toffoli-based
adder circuit per asset) compared with, e.g., a spectral method that would need
a fast quantum Fourier transform on every step.

\textbf{The norm bound.} The bound on $\|\hat H_d\|$ follows from bounding each
term separately: the finite-difference Laplacian's spectral radius is
$O(1/\Delta x^2)$ (standard finite-difference stability theory), and the
potential term is bounded by its maximum over the domain, $|x_a|\le L$. This
bound is what feeds directly into the query-complexity formula of Stage~8
(where $\alpha\ge\|H_S\|\propto\|\hat H_d\|$), so getting it right here
determines the resource count downstream; it is currently an estimate from
these notes and not yet checked against a direct numerical eigenvalue
computation.

\subsection*{Which formula is used, and why}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{3.3cm}L{3.6cm}L{6.9cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Formula} & \textbf{Equation} & \textbf{Why this one} \\
\midrule
$N_x=2^{n_x}$ register & --- & Matches qubit register size; avoids a
  non-power-of-two-to-qubit encoding overhead. \\
Central second-difference $D_{2,a}$ & \eqref{eq:disc-operator} & Preserves

\section{Stage 7 --- Schr\"odingerisation}
\label{sec:stage7}
% =====================================================================

\subsection*{What this stage needs}

The discrete Hermitian operator $\hat H_d$ from Stage~6. \textbf{This stage
must not proceed past the sketch below until the Jin--Liu--Yu papers (Section
\ref{sec:reading}) are actually read}; everything here is reconstructed from
memory and is explicitly a placeholder for the primary source's equations and
stated conditions.

\subsection*{Why this stage exists}

$A=-\hat H_d$ generates the evolution $\partial_s\psi=A\psi$, i.e.\ the
semigroup $e^{sA}$. Since $\hat H_d\ge0$ (sum of a nonnegative kinetic term and
a nonnegative quadratic potential, both bounded below), $A\le0$, so $e^{sA}$ is
a \emph{contraction}, not a rotation: it shrinks norms rather than preserving
them. A quantum computer's native primitive is unitary evolution $e^{-isH_S}$
for Hermitian $H_S$, which preserves norms exactly. Schr\"odingerisation (Jin,
Liu, Yu) is the published technique for embedding a norm-shrinking linear
evolution into a norm-preserving one on an enlarged Hilbert space, by adding an
auxiliary variable and Fourier/Laplace-transforming the evolution in that
variable. This is why it is used here rather than, e.g., a non-unitary
block-encoding simulated via amplitude amplification directly: Schr\"odingerisation
converts the \emph{entire} evolution to a genuine Schr\"odinger equation, after
which all the standard unitary Hamiltonian-simulation machinery (Stage~8)
applies without modification.

\subsection*{Sketch (from memory --- to be replaced)}

$A=-\hat H_d$ is Hermitian and negative semidefinite. Set $w=e^{-p}\psi$ for an
auxiliary variable $p>0$ (extended to $p<0$ by a specific published
prescription), and Fourier-transform in $p$ with frequencies $\eta_k$. The
claimed result is a Schr\"odinger equation
\begin{equation}
  i\,\partial_s\hat w=H_S\hat w,\qquad H_S=-\hat H_d\otimes D_\eta,\qquad
  \|H_S\|=\|\hat H_d\|\,\eta_{\max},\ \ \eta_{\max}=\pi/\Delta p
  \label{eq:schrodinger}
\end{equation}
and $\psi$ is recovered as $e^{p^*}w(\cdot,p^*)$ for any $p^*>0$ (the
exponential weight $e^{-p}$ in the substitution is precisely what turns the
decaying evolution into an oscillating one in the extended variable, which is
the mechanism by which unitarity is restored).

\subsection*{Which formula is used, and why (to be confirmed against the primary source)}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{3.3cm}L{3.6cm}L{6.9cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Formula} & \textbf{Equation} & \textbf{Why this one (claimed)} \\
\midrule
Auxiliary variable $w=e^{-p}\psi$ & --- & The exponential weight is what
  converts a decaying semigroup into a unitary (oscillating) one in the
  extended $(\mathbf x,p)$ space; this is the defining trick of
  Schr\"odingerisation. \\
Fourier transform in $p$ & --- & Diagonalises the new auxiliary direction,
  turning the extra dimension into a tensor factor $D_\eta$ acting as a
  momentum operator, so $H_S$ remains a sum of commuting pieces. \\
$\|H_S\|=\|\hat H_d\|\eta_{\max}$ & \eqref{eq:schrodinger} & Norm of a tensor
  product of two operators is the product of their norms; feeds directly into
  Stage~8's query bound $\alpha\ge\|H_S\|$. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection*{What must be derived from the papers (not assumed)}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item The precise conditions on the $p$-domain and the step $\Delta p$ under
        which the claimed equation~\eqref{eq:schrodinger} holds, and any error
        terms from truncating $p$ to a finite range.
  \item The success probability of the final recovery step (extracting $\psi$
        from $w$ at a fixed $p^*$), including the factor coming from the norm
        decay $e^{-E_0T}$ noted in Stage~5. A known constant shift of $\hat H$
        (adding a multiple of the identity) removes the bulk exponential decay
        without changing the gradient $\nabla\ln\psi$ (since a constant shift
        does not affect $\ln\psi$'s gradient), and this is the mechanism
        expected to control the success probability -- but the papers' exact
        statement of this must replace the claim here.
\end{itemize}

\section{Stage 8 --- Resource count}
\label{sec:stage8}
% =====================================================================

\subsection*{What this stage needs}

The unitary Schr\"odinger-form Hamiltonian $H_S$ and its norm bound
$\|H_S\|$ from Stage~7.

\subsection*{How it is to be achieved}

\paragraph{Qubits.}
\begin{equation}
  N_q=M\,n_x+n_p+n_{\mathrm{anc}}
  \label{eq:nq}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Why this count.} $M\,n_x$ qubits encode the $M$-asset position grid
(Stage~6: $n_x$ qubits per asset register, $M$ assets). $n_p=\log_2N_p$ encodes
the auxiliary Schr\"odingerisation variable $p$ (Stage~7) on its own discretised
grid of $N_p$ points. $n_{\mathrm{anc}}$ are ancillas consumed by whichever
block-encoding of $H_S$ is used (qubitization or QSVT); this count is specific
to the chosen block-encoding circuit and must be taken from that circuit's
construction, not estimated independently.

\paragraph{Queries to the block-encoding.}
\begin{equation}
  Q=O\!\big(\alpha T+\log(1/\varepsilon)/\log\log(1/\varepsilon)\big),
  \qquad \alpha\ge\|H_S\|
  \label{eq:queries}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Why this formula and not, e.g., Trotterisation's $O(T^2/\varepsilon)$ or
$O(\mathrm{poly}(T/\varepsilon))$ scaling.} Qubitization/QSVT-based Hamiltonian
simulation achieves query complexity scaling \emph{linearly} in simulated time
$T$ and only \emph{doubly-logarithmically} in $1/\varepsilon$ --- the
best known scaling in both parameters, and provably optimal in $T$ (no
simulation method can do better, by a no-fast-forwarding argument). Given that
this project's deliverable is explicitly a resource estimate (Stage~0: no
speedup is claimed), using the asymptotically best known method gives the
most meaningful (smallest, least pessimistic) lower bound on resources, which
is the fairest number to report. $\alpha$ must be an upper bound on the true
operator norm $\|H_S\|$ because qubitization requires normalising the
Hamiltonian into a block of a unitary, and the normalisation constant directly
multiplies the query count.

\paragraph{T-gates.}
\begin{equation}
  N_T=Q\times(\text{T-cost of one block-encoding})\ +\ (\text{rotation
  synthesis})
  \label{eq:nt}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Why this decomposition.} Each of the $Q$ queries to the block-encoding
costs a fixed number of T-gates set by the block-encoding circuit; multiplying
by $Q$ is just the total-cost-equals-cost-per-call-times-number-of-calls
identity. The additional rotation-synthesis term is needed separately because
qubitization/QSVT circuits typically require a sequence of single-qubit
rotations (e.g.\ for the QSVT phase angles) that are not natively
Clifford+T and must be synthesised to precision $\varepsilon$, at a cost that
scales as $O(\log(1/\varepsilon))$ per rotation (Ross--Selinger-type bounds)
and must be added, not folded into the per-query block-encoding cost.

\textbf{Within the block-encoding, per Stage~6:} the kinetic term
$-D_{2,a}$ is implemented with quantum adders/subtractors on $n_x$-bit
registers (standard reversible arithmetic circuits, $O(n_x)$ T-gates each,
since a ripple-carry or similar adder on $n_x$-bit registers costs linearly
many Toffolis); the potential term $x_ax_b$ (needed for $\mathbf x^\top
C\mathbf x$) costs $O(n_x^2)$ Toffoli gates per product, because a
binary multiplier of two $n_x$-bit registers is the standard
$O(n_x^2)$-gate schoolbook-multiplication circuit (faster multipliers exist
asymptotically but are not simpler at the small $n_x$ relevant here).

\paragraph{Cross-check.} The analytic count above should be cross-checked
against a circuit-level estimate from a resource-estimation tool (e.g.\
Azure Quantum Resource Estimator or an equivalent), since the analytic
formula uses asymptotic ($O(\cdot)$) notation and a tool gives the actual
constants for the chosen instance.

\subsection*{Which formula is used, and why (summary)}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{3.3cm}L{3.6cm}L{6.9cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Formula} & \textbf{Equation} & \textbf{Why this one} \\
\midrule
$N_q=Mn_x+n_p+n_{\mathrm{anc}}$ & \eqref{eq:nq} & Direct sum of the registers
  introduced in Stages 6--7 plus the block-encoding's own ancilla count. \\
Qubitization/QSVT query count & \eqref{eq:queries} & Best known scaling in both

T-gates $=Q\times$cost + rotations & \eqref{eq:nt} & Separates the

\section{Stage 9 --- Readout}
\label{sec:stage9}
% =====================================================================

\subsection*{What this stage needs}

The simulated state $w$ (or $\psi$, recovered per Stage~7) at $s=T$, and the
$N_T$ formula structure from Stage~8, into which the readout cost must be
added.

\subsection*{How it is to be achieved}

\textbf{What is extracted.} The quantity of actual interest is the trading
rate $\mathbf v^*(\mathbf X,0)=-2\nu H^{-1}\nabla\ln\psi(\mathbf X)$
(Stage~4), which requires $\nabla\ln\psi$ at $\mathbf x=\mathbf X$, i.e.\ the
\emph{ratio} of amplitudes at neighbouring grid points (since
$\nabla\ln\psi\approx[\ln\psi(\mathbf X+\Delta x\,\mathbf e_j)-\ln\psi(\mathbf
X)]/\Delta x$ from a finite difference consistent with Stage~6's
discretisation).

\textbf{Why amplitude estimation.} The amplitude of a specific computational
basis state $|\mathbf X\rangle$ in the output state $\psi$ cannot be read
directly from a single measurement (a single shot gives only a bit string,
with probability equal to the squared amplitude, not the amplitude or its
phase/sign). Amplitude estimation is the standard quantum primitive for
converting that squared-probability information into an estimate of the
amplitude itself to precision $\varepsilon$, using $O(1/\varepsilon)$ repeated
applications of the state-preparation circuit --- a quadratic improvement over
the $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ repetitions that plain sampling would need to reach
the same precision. Because $\psi(\mathbf X)$ may be exponentially small
(Stage~5: $\psi(\mathbf X)\sim e^{-W(\mathbf X)/4\nu}$), the absolute number of
repetitions needed to resolve it to a fixed \emph{relative} precision is what
must be stated explicitly, since this is identified in the Risks
(Section~\ref{sec:risks}) as the term most likely to dominate $N_T$.

\textbf{Cost formula (to state explicitly).} The readout cost should be
expressed as a function of the target precision $\varepsilon$ and of
$\psi(\mathbf X)$ as tabulated in Stage~5, since the number of amplitude-estimation
repetitions needed to resolve a quantity of size $\psi(\mathbf X)$ to relative
precision $\varepsilon$ scales as $O(1/(\varepsilon\,\psi(\mathbf X)))$ (smaller
signal needs proportionally more repetitions to resolve at the same relative
accuracy), and each repetition costs one call to the full Schr\"odingerised
simulation circuit from Stages 7--8.

\subsection*{Which formula is used, and why}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{3.3cm}L{3.6cm}L{6.9cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Formula} & \textbf{Equation} & \textbf{Why this one} \\
\midrule
Amplitude estimation, $O(1/\varepsilon)$ repetitions & --- & Quadratically
  fewer repetitions than direct sampling for a fixed target precision;
  standard primitive for extracting an amplitude (not just a probability). \\
Finite-difference gradient at $\mathbf X$ & --- & Reuses the same grid spacing
  $\Delta x$ already fixed in Stage~6, so no separate discretisation choice is
  introduced for the readout. \\
Repetition count $\propto 1/(\varepsilon\,\psi(\mathbf X))$ & --- & Resolving a

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{Risks}\label{sec:risks}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Cole--Hopf claim unchecked.} Everything after Stage~4 rests on
        the matrix Hopf--Cole substitution~$\psi=e^{-W/(4\nu)}$ actually
        linearising the HJB equation as claimed; see the Stage~4 key claim.
  \item \textbf{Schr\"odingerisation steps from memory.} The Stage~7 sketch is

        before Stage~8's norm bound $\alpha\ge\|H_S\|$ can be trusted.
  \item \textbf{Prior work.} The closest paper found (Schr\"odingerization with
        Hamilton--Jacobi-type problems, arXiv:2606.28752) has not been read.
        No claim of novelty should be made before it is.
  \item \textbf{Stiffness.} A large terminal penalty $\Phi$ (Stage~2, A10)
        affects the conditioning of the Riccati solution $P(t)$ and therefore
        the norm bound in Stage~6 and the gate count in Stage~8.
  \item \textbf{Readout cost} (Stage~9) may dominate the total $N_T$, since it
        scales as $1/(\varepsilon\,\psi(\mathbf X))$ and $\psi(\mathbf X)$ can
        be exponentially small (Stage~5).
  \item \textbf{Classical triviality.} A reviewer will point out the
        closed-form Riccati solution (Stage~2); the framing in Stage~0 (Goal)
        answers this directly: no speedup is claimed, only a resource count.
\end{itemize}
